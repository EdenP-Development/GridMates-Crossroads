# Tabletop and mobile-game research — living reference
**Reviewed:** 2026-11-16. Crossroads should borrow general design lessons, not protected illustrations, naming, text, or branded gameplay presentation.

## Reference: The Game of Life (Hasbro)
Official descriptions emphasize journey choices, winding movement, unexpected action/card events, and family play. The latest product description includes decisions between paths and short unexpected encounters.
- https://instructions.hasbro.com/en-us/instruction/the-game-of-life-game
- https://instructions.hasbro.com/en-us/instruction/the-game-of-life

**Borrow:** branch decisions, memorable event prompts, one shared journey, approachable tabletop pacing.
**Avoid:** career/family/money bookkeeping, mandatory card reading, life simulation, matching board art or branding.

## Reference genres to compare in playtests
- **Ludo/Pachisi:** recognizable quick turn-taking, opponent capture and tempo swings; downside is too much luck and repeated starts.
- **Farkle:** risk/reward built from just dice; downside is repeated numeric scoring and limited spatial tactics.
- **Royal Game of Ur:** race with captures and dynamic positioning; downside for this concept is mainly two-player structure.
- **Shut the Box:** tiny, learnable turn loop; downside is little journey/geography.
- **Children's maze games:** readability and learnable intersections; avoid frustrating hidden dead ends or ambiguous route arrows.

## Crossroads' differentiation hypothesis
Fixed recognizable road networks + area-limited random event content + meaningful decisions at many forks + quick first-to-finish race + light social mischief. The game should create a memory of *why a route was chosen*, not rely on long text or novelty art.

## Questions for first 12 playtests
- Did the player make at least one meaningful decision per race?
- Was any route clearly dominant irrespective of player position?
- Was a loss understandable, and did players want a rematch?
- Was targeting amusing, or did someone feel bullied?
- Did total play time fit Quick/Classic/Long expectations on 2/3/4 seats?
- How many event prompts were too many?
- Did players feel rewarded for learning the board, without event outcomes becoming predictable?
